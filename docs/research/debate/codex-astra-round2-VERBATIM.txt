1. DISTRIBUTIONS — PARTIAL.

Claude is right that an “unknown” enum does not create reliable abstention. Deterministic code acting on a hard label does not make that label correct either. My round-one proposal supplied an output contract, not a calibrated uncertainty mechanism.

But ranking and asymmetric thresholds do not inherently require logprobs:

- Ranking can use deterministic relevance scores, constrained ordinal grades, or pairwise A/B/tie comparisons. Pairwise comparisons introduce cost, order effects and potentially inconsistent rankings.
- Abstention can follow missing evidence, failed predicates, disagreement across samples, or disagreement between independently grounded checks.
- Five-sample self-consistency supplies an empirical agreement score. It is neither a calibrated probability nor necessarily cheaper than obtaining logits once.

The cited audit actually demonstrates the last alternative: k=5 improved selective factual accuracy from 42.7% to 82.8%, at only 28.2% coverage. It also explicitly limits generalisation beyond the tested model/configuration. This is evidence against trusting verbalised confidence, not evidence that distributions are indispensable. [Primary paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.23663v2)

Logprobs deserve a benchmark arm. They are probabilities of model outputs, not automatically probabilities that decisions are correct. Calibration and held-out evaluation remain necessary.

My revised position: an enum is sufficient for the interface; the deployment may need an additional uncertainty signal. Neither Claude nor I can settle the cheapest adequate signal by argument. Also, requiring two successful workloads before generalising remains reasonable; requiring two before trying logits on one worthwhile workload would be needless obstruction.

2. SEQUENTIAL RELIABILITY — AGREE.

I accept Claude’s correction. The illustrative 0.98^20 ≈ 0.668 applies regardless of the selector’s price. It argues against treating per-step accuracy as task reliability, not against cheap selectors.

The calculation assumes independent errors and that every erroneous step defeats completion. Recovery changes that model; correlated misunderstandings can make matters worse.

Deterministic postconditions should verify observable progress after each meaningful action, with bounded recovery. They cannot prove every semantic choice correct: typing the wrong recipient can pass a field-value check perfectly.

Compare selectors under the same grounding, verification and recovery system. Measure completed tasks, harmful actions, interventions, latency and total cost. My round-one argument supports that comparison; it does not establish its winner.

4. DID CLAUDE CONCEDE TOO MUCH?

(a) Hosted Jev and public pages

A blanket rejection is unjustified for genuinely public page content. The revised “no hosted Jev for private state” boundary is defensible; “public fixtures only, never a real public-page workload” is unnecessarily restrictive.

A public-page comparison can exclude cookies, authenticated DOM, unpublished drafts, private analytics and internal business instructions. Ownership of the website does not make all associated data public.

Privacy then stops being the decisive objection. Incremental quality, cost, latency and dependency become the questions. Claude should defend a public-content benchmark arm, not assume hosted adoption or permanent exclusion. The original document already allowed non-private evaluation fixtures.

(b) Memory forget/export first

Keep them as basic safeguards. Withdraw the implication that comprehensive forgetting is an obviously cheap prerequisite to the browser experiment.

Export is bounded only after defining its scope. Effective forgetting may require deleting derived descriptions and embeddings, invalidating caches, handling retained logs and backups, and preventing re-ingestion from surviving sources. A delete button is not that contract.

Deliver a narrowly defined export/delete capability with explicit coverage alongside the useful workflow. Do not quietly turn “memory controls first” into another infrastructure programme. Claude was right to retain user control; neither of us established its implementation cost.

(c) Randomised false-sleep audits

The sampling idea is right. “System Two judges, therefore unbiased false-sleep estimate” is wrong.

Uniform random selection of would-be skips removes selection bias for the sampled skip population, assuming audits actually complete and labels concern the same pre-wake state. It estimates P(should wake | would skip), not P(skip | should wake), which needs a different denominator.

System Two supplies a proxy label. Shared model errors, missing context and hindsight can bias it. Human adjudication of a sample, or objective outcome checks, are still needed. If forced wakes change later state, the estimate describes the audited operating policy, not automatically the counterfactual system that never audits.

The proposed volume is weak:

- At 5%, fifty audits require 1,000 would-be skips in expectation: 16.7 daily over sixty days.
- One three-hour monitor produces at most 480 opportunities in sixty days, yielding at most 24 expected audits if every opportunity would skip.
- Fifty independent audits with zero false sleeps give an approximately 5.8% one-sided 95% upper bound. They cannot establish a 1% tolerance; that would require about 299 zero-failure audits.

Repeated ticks of the same condition also provide less independent evidence than fifty distinct events.

Choose audit volume from the tolerated error and observed traffic. “5% and fifty” is a pilot budget, not a reliability gate.

(d) Adversary stop rule

Not sensible as written. If the red-team set supplies positives, review them before deployment. Waiting thirty days to accumulate thirty seeded positives measures scheduling, not field evidence.

Thirty positives also say little without attack diversity and held-out separation. Variants of one exploit are not thirty independent security challenges.

Use a fixed reviewed development set and a separate held-out challenge set. Report seeded attack recall separately from natural-traffic false blocks and review burden. Reviewing only disagreements and blocks misses attacks both models allow; sample agreements too. The incumbent verdict is a comparison target, not ground truth.

A three-hour review budget is a defensible project constraint. Exceeding it should end or redesign the experimental classifier, not remove the existing protection or restore fail-open behaviour.

I read the entire constant, including all three examples at librarian.rs:45–77.

Beyond the invented year:

- The task example substitutes documentation for docs in both FACTS and TERMS. This is abbreviation expansion, not inflection.
- That example drops “via claude-code,” losing explicit provenance. This is an omission, not an invented fact.

Google services and software development illustrate semantic classification beyond copied vocabulary. Such categories can be useful; they should be explicitly permitted as categories, not silently treated as extracted facts.

The third example does not clearly invent task completion: its input already says “Task completed.” I would not charge it with that.

The smallest coherent repair is:

- Demand one JSON object containing exactly FACTS, TERMS and CATEGORIES.
- FACTS must preserve explicit identifiers, dates, attribution and qualifications; do not resolve unknown dates or entities using background knowledge.
- TERMS contains source vocabulary and explicitly permitted morphological forms; no synonyms or implicit brand expansions.
- CATEGORIES may contain broad supported classifications, with no new concrete facts.
- Remove or rewrite every example to follow those rules. Permit shorter arrays when evidence is sparse, and align the parser’s minimum-count checks accordingly.

For native Ollama, pass a JSON Schema through top-level format in InferenceBody::for_stream() at pool.rs:473, and top-level think:false for models supporting that setting. The schema should require exactly those three keys, disallow additionalProperties, type FACTS as a string and the other fields as string arrays, and bound their lengths. Do not hide format or think inside options. [Ollama generate API](https://docs.ollama.com/api/generate)

Pass the schema explicitly from the Librarian caller; do not impose it globally on shared builders or adjudication calls with different contracts. On the compatible path at pool.rs:492, use response_format with the equivalent JSON Schema where supported. [Ollama structured outputs](https://docs.ollama.com/capabilities/structured-outputs)

The existing JSON parser accepts arrays, but also accepts alternate shapes and ignores extra fields. Strict validation must precede it.

Schema enforcement guarantees structure, not factual fidelity, one-sentence semantics or a tokenizer-specific thirty-token limit. Those remain separate checks. The Apple-first route also needs its own contract enforcement; fixing Ollama does not fix every backend.

FINAL JOINT POSITION

The five positions already supported by both arguments are:

1. Fix concrete correctness defects before constructing a general decision platform.
2. Short-output spending is not established waste; measure call purpose and pre-call evidence before claiming savings.
3. Test one repeated browser workflow early, with adequate context, postconditions, bounded recovery and consequential-action approval.
4. Repair the Librarian contract and examples before comparing models; judge faithful extraction and the backend actually served.
5. Keep authorisation deterministic and private state local; reject classifier-granted permissions and causal SEO claims from ordinary before/after traffic.

Three questions remain experimental:

1. On an actual ranking or selective-decision workload, which signal wins at the required error tolerance: deterministic scoring, constrained comparisons, repeated sampling or calibrated logits?
